## Why is the sky blue?

Why is the sky blue?

The answer to this question lies in a combination of three factors: radiation, cloud cover and atmospheric transparency.

What causes the sky to be blue?

A great deal of it depends on how much sunlight reaches our atmosphere. The amount of light that reaches us depends not just upon where we live but also upon what kind of clouds are present at the time. When there’s no clouds around, white light will hit all surfaces equally. This results in a bright yellow or red hue. If the surface has lots of particles in the air, however, they scatter the light (causing it to disperse), giving us dark blue skies.

How do you explain why the sky looks blue when the sun is shining directly over your head?

Is the sky blue for all seasons?

When the sky appears blue during different times of the year, it doesn’t necessarily mean the temperature isn’t changing; it means the season itself changes too! In wintertime, the sky tends to look lighter than summer because less solar energy hits Earth compared to when the Sun is higher up in the sky. As such, it gets darker – meaning more of the sunlight falls onto the ground instead of being reflected back into space.

The winners were Professor Abraham Kahan, of Columbia University, and Professor Robert Jendru, of Stanford University. Their work focused on measuring how a nation’s economic growth compares with its standard of living over time.

In their research, Kahan and Jendru estimated that countries can be categorized as poor or rich based on whether they have higher per capita income relative to the rest of the world. As such, these societies are characterized by low levels of inequality—meaning there is little disparity between those who earn more money than others and those who don’t. On the other hand, wealthy nations tend to enjoy high levels of equality because resources are shared widely across society.

Kahan and Jendru studied data from 156 developed countries for six years starting in 1987 and then extended their analysis into seven years following their initial publication in 1993. They found that while some parts of the world consistently show lower average incomes compared to richer counterparts worldwide (such as China), overall performance remained relatively stable throughout most of the study period.
